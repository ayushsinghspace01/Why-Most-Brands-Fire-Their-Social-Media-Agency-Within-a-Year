# Why-Most-Brands-Fire-Their-Social-Media-Agency-Within-a-Year
The pattern is consistent enough to be predictable.

Month one is enthusiastic. A content calendar arrives, the grid starts looking coherent, posting becomes regular for the first time in years. Month four, someone in a leadership meeting asks what this is producing. Month seven, the reporting has quietly become a slide of follower counts and reach figures. Month eleven, the contract is not renewed and both parties privately blame the other.

Very little of this is about creative quality. It is about how the engagement was scoped, and the scoping mistakes are the same ones almost every time.
Mistake one: buying posts instead of buying an outcome
Most social media proposals are priced in units of content. Twelve posts a month, four reels, eight stories, one carousel a week.

This is a comfortable way to buy, because it looks like value you can count. It is also the root of the problem, because the number of posts has almost no relationship to the outcome anyone actually wants.

A brand publishing twelve forgettable posts a month is not ahead of one publishing four that people respond to. The unit of production has become the deliverable, and once that happens both sides start optimising for filling the calendar rather than for anything else.

A better scoping conversation starts from a different question: what is social supposed to do for this business? The honest answers vary and they are not interchangeable.

Generating demand directly
Supporting sales conversations already in progress
Recruitment and employer brand
Customer service and community
Category authority over a long horizon

Each of those implies a different content approach, a different platform mix, a different measurement framework, and a different resourcing model. Scoping without answering this produces a calendar that half-serves several goals and fully serves none.
Mistake two: treating organic and paid as one budget line
These are different disciplines with different economics and they are frequently bundled into a single retainer where the split is never examined.

Organic social is slow, compounding, and increasingly limited by reach. On most platforms, meaningful unpaid reach beyond your existing followers is difficult and getting harder. What organic does well is deepen a relationship with people already aware of you and give sales something credible to point at.

Paid social buys reach and can be measured properly. It also stops working the day you stop paying.

Problems arise when a client expects paid outcomes from an organic budget. Twelve well-made posts a month to an audience of four thousand will not generate pipeline, and no amount of creative quality changes that arithmetic. If demand generation is the goal, there has to be a media budget, and it needs to be separate and visible rather than absorbed into a retainer.

Ask any prospective agency to split their proposal explicitly: production, community management, paid media management, and the media spend itself. Four numbers, not one.
Mistake three: an approval process slower than the platform
This is the most underestimated failure mode and it is almost always the client's fault.

Social rewards responsiveness. A relevant post published the day something happens performs very differently from the same post published eleven days later after three rounds of internal review.

Many brands run a three-stage approval cycle involving marketing, product, and occasionally legal, with each stage taking two to four days. Under that structure, timely content is structurally impossible. What survives is safe, evergreen, and forgettable, because that is the only content type that can withstand a two-week approval lag.

If you cannot change the approval process, be honest about it and scope accordingly. Do not commission a reactive social strategy and then run it through a workflow that guarantees it cannot be reactive.

If you can change it, the highest-value fix is usually a pre-approved territory: defined topics and formats a named person can publish without further review, with the full process reserved for anything outside those boundaries.
Mistake four: nobody owns community management
Content production and community management are different jobs, and proposals routinely bundle the second in as an afterthought.

Community management means responding to comments, handling direct messages, moderating, and being present in conversations happening elsewhere. It requires availability rather than scheduled effort, and it does not fit neatly into a monthly deliverable count.

Left unscoped, it either does not happen, which makes your account look abandoned in the replies, or it lands unofficially on someone junior at the client who has eleven other priorities.

Decide explicitly: who responds, within what timeframe, with what authority to speak, and what gets escalated. Then price it separately, because it is a separate service.
Mistake five: measuring what is easy rather than what matters
Follower count and reach dominate social reporting because they are available, they usually go up, and they fill a slide.

They are also close to useless as a basis for a renewal decision, and everyone in the room knows it, which is why the month-seven conversation happens.

More useful, in rough order of effort:

Engaged audience rather than total audience. How many accounts interact more than once, rather than how many follow.

Traffic and its behaviour. Not just sessions from social, but what those visitors do afterwards.

Assisted conversions. Social rarely closes anything directly in B2B. It appears earlier in the path.

Sales usage. Ask the sales team monthly which posts or assets they have actually sent to prospects. Costs nothing, tells you more than any dashboard.

Share of conversation. In defined categories, how often your brand appears relative to competitors in relevant discussion.

Recruitment signal. If employer brand is part of the goal, candidate quality and source data belong in the report.

Agree these at the start. Attempting to renegotiate the measurement framework in month six, when someone is already unhappy, is a negotiation rather than a review.
What to actually ask a prospective agency
The questions that separate a production vendor from a partner:

What is this supposed to achieve, and how will we know?
Which platforms would you drop, and why? An agency that recommends being everywhere has not thought about it.
Who does community management, and what are their response time commitments?
How do you handle approvals, and what do you need from us to move quickly?
Show me a campaign that underperformed and explain what you learned.
What does your reporting look like in month six, not month one?

That last one matters more than people expect. Reporting enthusiasm decays predictably, and asking about it up front sets an expectation.

When working through shortlists and roundups of [digital marketing agencies](https://spacebar.in/best-digital-marketing-agencies-in-india/), it is worth separating firms that treat social as a bundled line item inside a broader retainer from those where it is a genuine specialism. Both models can work, but the first tends to produce competent calendar-filling, and the second tends to produce work someone actually notices.

Where social is the primary channel for your business, evaluating [social media marketing agencies](https://spacebar.in/social-media-marketing-services-in-india/) on the depth of their community management and paid media capability, rather than on the strength of their creative portfolio alone, is usually the better filter. Portfolios show what an agency can make. They do not show whether anyone responded.
The short version
Scope the outcome, not the post count. Split organic from paid, and both from media spend. Fix your approval process or admit it is fixed. Price community management as its own service. Agree measurement before month one.

Do that and the month-eleven conversation tends not to happen, because everyone has been looking at the same thing since the start.
